The whole thing in one page

If you read nothing else, read this.

What we set out to do. Modern segmentation models are asked to label every pixel of a satellite image with a class name — building, water, forest. The best training‑free method for this, SegEarth‑OV3, has a rule: if the model is not confident enough about a pixel, throw that pixel into a bin called "background". We measured that bin. On LoveDA it holds 29.68% of all the real land‑cover pixels in the dataset — 323 million of them. Our plan was to rescue them, using the fact that land cover has strong spatial grammar: roads touch buildings, buildings essentially never touch water.

What happened. The rescue plan failed, and it failed for a reason we could measure precisely. But while proving it failed, we found something better and simpler that works: the confidence threshold itself is the wrong shape. One threshold is used for all classes, and it is wrong for different classes in opposite directions. Fitting one threshold per class — using the same labelled data the baseline already spends tuning its single threshold, training no new weights — recovers a real gain.

Why this matters more than a bigger number would

The gain is larger on the pipeline it was not developed for, and on that pipeline every single class improves. So the finding is not "SAM 3 has a quirk". It is a statement about any method that thresholds per‑class confidence scores — which is nearly all of them. And it composes with our nearest published competitor instead of beating it: their score goes 36.99 → 39.52 when we put our calibration on top. We improve their method rather than compete with it.

The second half of the paper is a measurement study explaining why the residual exists and why rescuing it does not work. The short answer is that it is governed by how the dataset was labelled, not by the model. When a dataset provides a "catch‑all" class that means everything else, the model always has a plausible answer available, and the discarded pixels become impossible to distinguish from genuine background. We proved that causally with a controlled experiment, and then a third dataset falsified half of it — which we report as a falsification rather than repair.

Act I — The wrong PDF

11–12 August · commits da2bb6f → 3cb1edb

The project began with a problem statement and two PDFs. The statement proposed an unsupervised pipeline: run a segmentation model, keep the confident pixels, build a matrix of which classes sit next to which, and use that matrix to label the unconfident pixels. Sensible. The repository at that point held a scaffold built on SAM 1 + CLIP.

Two things were wrong before a single line was run, and finding them in week one instead of week nine is most of why the project survived.

1. The second PDF was not the SAM paper

The file named sam_research_papaer(1).pdf was actually SegEarth‑OV3 (arXiv:2512.08730) — written by the same group that wrote the first PDF (SegEarth‑OV, CVPR 2025). It already does a large part of what the problem statement proposed, using SAM 3 rather than SAM 1.

The realisation

We had accidentally been handed our own closest competitor, and had been planning to rebuild half of it from scratch on an older model.

This was good news, not bad, and the reasoning matters:

  • The baseline is published and open source. We do not have to build from zero.
  • The question "does SAM 3 even work on satellite images?" is already answered. That saves a month.
  • We now know exactly what our contribution has to be in order not to be a duplicate.

So the project was repositioned on day two. Not "an unsupervised SAM 3 pipeline", but: SegEarth‑OV3 throws away low‑confidence pixels into background; we recover them. SegEarth‑OV3 became our named baseline, and our headline number became a delta against it rather than an unanchored score.

2. "Unsupervised" was the wrong word

The input includes a list of the possible class names. That is not unsupervised — it is zero‑shot / open‑vocabulary / training‑free. SegEarth‑OV itself footnotes that even "training‑free" is a stretch for them, and settles on annotation‑free. We adopted the same careful phrasing: training‑free, annotation‑free open‑vocabulary semantic segmentation. Examiners and reviewers notice this.

Then: does the idea have any signal at all?

Before writing any method, we wrote a 40‑line script (cooccurrence_gt.py) that measured, from ground‑truth masks alone, whether land‑cover classes actually have adjacency structure. The measure is pointwise mutual information (PMI): how much more often do two classes touch than they would if they were scattered at random?

MeasurementResult
Mean |PMI| over class pairs, LoveDA urban1.658 bits
Mean |PMI| over class pairs, LoveDA rural1.538 bits
Random‑scatter control (what "no signal" looks like)0.004 bits

Hundreds of times above the noise floor. The premise held. Three design decisions came straight out of that first measurement, and all three overturned what the original statement proposed:

Original designReplaced by, and why
Adjacency = distance between region centresShared boundary length — two regions can be close without touching.
Add up positive evidence from neighboursSigned PMI — the largest signals are negative. Building–water is 0.02× chance. "This borders a building, so it is almost certainly not water" is the single most reliable fact available, and a positive‑only formula cannot express it.
One matrix per imageHierarchical mix of a corpus‑wide matrix and a per‑image one — 6 of 15 class pairs flip sign between urban and rural. A single global matrix averages them into mush.

And a second competitor appeared

ConInfer (arXiv:2603.29271, March 2026) claims to be the first work to use context at inference time for this exact task, reporting +2.80 mIoU over SegEarth‑OV. Any reviewer in the area would know it. But it did not close our gap, and we wrote down precisely why:

GranularityPatch levelRegion level
Where the prior comes fromFitted per sceneMined across the corpus

Their own limitations section names pixel‑ and region‑level context modelling as future work. That was our opening.

Act II — The week the environment nearly won

17–18 August · commits f18ad72 → 9eea352

Nothing in this act is intellectually interesting and it cost the most calendar time of any single obstacle. It is included because it is the honest answer to "why did week two take a week".

The three‑way version deadlock

SegEarth‑OV3 is built on MMSegmentation, which needs a compiled library called mmcv. Three requirements intersect at exactly one point in the whole version space:

ComponentConstraint
SAM 3needs torch ≥ 2.3 (it uses an API that did not exist before)
mmcv prebuilt wheelsexist for torch 2.1–2.4, and none for 2.5+
mmsegmentation 1.2.2asserts mmcv < 2.2.0

So torch 2.4.1 is the only version that satisfies SAM 3 and still has a prebuilt mmcv. Its wheel is mmcv 2.2.0 — excluded by MMSegmentation's upper bound by one patch version. The fix was a one‑character edit to an installed package (MMCV_MAX = '2.2.0' → '2.3.0').

Five other approaches were tried and all failed:

  • Python 3.13 — no mmcv wheels exist for it at all.
  • mmcv-lite — MMSegmentation eagerly imports compiled CUDA operations at package load, so the lite build cannot work. Installing both breaks imports entirely.
  • Building mmcv from source — the system CUDA compiler is version 13.3 while torch is built against 12.1, and torch refuses to compile across that gap.
  • Installing the CUDA toolkit into the environment — the package that provides the compiler is a different one, so this quietly does nothing.
  • Newer mmcv from source — its installer imports a module that modern setuptools removed.
Standing rule since

Never run pip install -U in this environment, and never rebuild it by hand. The working combination is captured in scripts/setup_env.sh, and the project's instruction file states it in bold at the top. Every number in the project rests on it.

Read the two highlighted rows together. Water: right nine times in ten when it fires, but finds only half the water present. That told us where any gain would have to come from, seventeen days before we found the method that delivered it.

Act III — Measuring the motivation, and refuting ourselves twice

20–21 August · commits df78a97 → 21f749e

The premise needed a number, not an argument. We wrote instrumentation that records, for every tile, what fraction of real land‑cover pixels the baseline assigns to background.

The headline motivation number

29.68% of all real‑class pixels in LoveDA validation are assigned to background — 323,184,908 pixels. The pre‑agreed rule was that anything below 5% would mean pivoting the project. It cleared that bar by a factor of six.

The obvious objection, answered before a reviewer could raise it

Anyone reading that number asks the same thing immediately: why not just lower the threshold? So we ran it, at three settings.

Threshold τmIoUReal pixels discarded
0.5 (the published setting)47.3729.68%

Lowering the threshold recovers two‑thirds of the discarded pixels and costs 5.54 mIoU. Tracking a single class through the sweep shows why: going from 0.5 to 0.1 recovers 85 million agricultural pixels and creates 148 million new false positives — 1.73 wrong for every 1 right.

And the cost is sharply non‑linear, which is a subtlety we would have missed with a two‑point sweep:

SegmentPixels recoveredmIoU costCost per 100M px
0.5 → 0.399.4M−0.730.73
0.3 → 0.1105.3M−4.814.57

Nearly identical pixel counts; the second half costs 6.2 times more. There is a shallow band just under the threshold that is cheap to reach, and a deep tail that is not. Any claim of the form "threshold relaxation is expensive" has to say which part of the range, or a reviewer with the middle row will object.

The dominant error is silence, not error

Every class's pixels end up in one of three places: correct, dumped in background, or confused with a different real class. Aggregated:

3 : 1

About 29.7% of real‑class pixels go to background against 9.9% confused with another real class. The baseline is mostly not wrong — it is silent. That single ratio is the answer to "why not just improve the classifier", and it leads the results section of the paper.

Self‑refutation #1: the presence head

SAM 3 has a "presence head" that outputs one number per class per image: does this concept exist here at all? SegEarth‑OV3 multiplies every pixel score by it. We probed a tile that reported 100% discard, and found something that looked spectacular:

Class on tile 3487Presence scoreDense evidenceBest possible score
road0.0757+10.13 (near‑certain)0.076
forest0.0094+5.030.009
agricultural0.0200+5.440.020

The dense part of the model is certain road is present. One global scalar crushes the whole tile below any usable threshold. It generalised, too: across all 1,669 tiles, catastrophic tiles had median presence 0.273 against 0.918 for healthy ones, correlation −0.750.

This looked like the strongest result in the project. We wrote down, in advance, what would count as proof and what would count as refutation, then ran the counterfactual: turn the presence gate off entirely.

Tile groupDiscard beforeAfterChange
catastrophic (198 tiles)99.97%60.82%−39.15
healthy (77 tiles)0.46%54.11%+53.64
Refuted — by us

Overall mIoU fell 11.97. And the disqualifying number was not that one: the correlation between a tile's presence score and how much it recovered was +0.018. If gating were suppressing recoverable evidence, the lowest‑presence tiles would recover most. It is zero. The tiles that recover are not the presence‑suppressed ones. Presence collapse is a correlate, not a cause. No causal claim about it went into the paper.

The mechanism turned out to be something else entirely, and better. SAM 3 essentially never detects "background" — median presence 0.022, against 0.45–0.91 for every real class. Obvious in hindsight: background is not a visual concept, it is the dataset's catch‑all for unlabelled area. So gating was holding background down. Remove it and background surges and wins outright.

This is worth a sentence in the paper on its own, because it inverts SegEarth‑OV3's stated motivation. They present presence gating as preventing hallucination of absent classes. Measured on LoveDA, its single largest effect is suppressing the background class. Their paper does not report this, and unlike the causal claim we abandoned, this one is directly measured.

Self‑refutation #2: "road is a hub" was measuring road's perimeter

Our own published analysis had found that roads attract every class, and had built a weighting scheme on it. Then we checked the statistics rather than the conclusion.

The PMI formula compares an observed quantity against an expected one. We were observing boundary frequency but computing the expectation from area. These are different measures. A class shaped like thin ribbons has far more boundary per unit area than one in fat blocks — so the formula was rewarding high‑perimeter classes for their geometry, with no semantics involved. Road is the thinnest class in LoveDA.

PairArea‑based (published)Boundary‑based (correct)Verdict
road – barren+2.32+0.17collapses
road – forest+1.96+0.06collapses
barren – forest+0.72−1.11flips sign
building – water−4.66−2.83survives

The premise survived; the ranking did not. Mean signal recomputed correctly is 0.574 bits against a 0.003 permutation floor — 216×, still decisive. But "road is a hub" was an artefact, and the real hub is agriculture. We verified the diagnosis on synthetic data: a corpus where one class is a thin ribbon by construction gives it +3.00 under the wrong formula and +0.23 under the right one.

This correction generalises beyond our project — anyone doing co‑occurrence analysis on segmentation masks will hit it — and it became one of the paper's secondary contributions.

Two discard mechanisms, not one

A pixel can end up labelled background two ways: it fell below the threshold, or background genuinely won the vote. We separated them exactly.

MechanismPixelsShare
(A) fell below the threshold303,706,23894.0%
(B) background won the vote outright19,378,1776.0%

Every single (B) pixel is water, concentrated in 24 tiles — large open‑water scenes. And (B) is unreachable by any threshold whatsoever, because lowering a threshold cannot change which class won a vote. Part of the residual lies outside what threshold tuning can address at all. That sharpened the motivation from "the obvious fix is expensive" to "and some of it the obvious fix cannot touch".

The motivation section, complete

Three blunt interventions, all measured, all failing: lower the threshold (−5.54, 1.73 wrong per right) · remove presence gating (−11.97, no net recovery) · do nothing (29.68% discarded). Every global knob that reaches the residual costs more than it returns, so any recovery has to be selective and semantic. Two refutations make a stronger motivation than one.

Act IV — The thesis dies

25–27 August · commits dc05235 → 286b643

This act builds the thing the project was founded on, tests it properly, and finds it does not earn its place. It is the most important act, because everything good came after it.

The prior was built, and it is accurate

We mined the co‑occurrence matrix from SAM 3's own confident predictions, using no labels — the honest, deployable version. The worry (written down back in Act I) was circularity: if the model is systematically unsure about barren, the mined matrix under‑represents every barren relationship, and we then use that matrix to label the barren regions the model missed. The prior would work against the classes that need help most.

Validation gateResult
Gate 1 — circularity✓ Passes. Mined matrix agrees with the ground‑truth matrix at ρ = +0.757. Error does not concentrate on the discarded classes (ρ = −0.257). The concern is retired with a number.
Gate 2 — usefulness✗ Fails, and against ground truth too.

Gate 2 is the interesting failure. It asked: does the matrix assign low compatibility to exactly the pairs the baseline confuses? The baseline's top confusion is forest predicted as agriculture. But the true matrix says forest and agriculture are attracted (+0.32). A perfect matrix would reinforce the error.

The reason is simple and fatal: adjacency and confusability are the same signal. Things that touch each other look alike at the boundary. This is not a mining defect that better engineering could fix.

The ceiling test

We then asked the fairest possible question: given regions we know should be relabelled, how well does the prior label them, compared to the dumbest possible alternative?

The thesis is dead

A perfect co‑occurrence matrix adds 0.3 points over copying the largest neighbour. Not the mined one — the perfect one. There is no engineering effort that recovers this. It works only where four or more classes border a region, which is 10% of the residual, diluting to +0.40 overall.

Verdict: it is an ablation row, not a thesis. And we do not revive it.

What actually mattered instead: the shape of the regions

While testing the prior, we tested the regions it operates on. This turned out to be worth sixty times more.

The largest connected component was an entire image tile — one region containing several different classes, so no single label could ever be right for it. Switching to SLIC lifted the ceiling by 20 points, against 0.3 for a perfect prior. Pixels in unlabelable regions fell from 12.5% to 1.1%.

And the real blocker: we cannot tell which pixels to fix

Relabelling is only useful if you know which background pixels deserve it. We tested nine different signals for that, scored by AUC — 0.5 is a coin flip, 1.0 is perfect.

All at chance. Three hypotheses died here: there is no hidden second threshold in the confidence scores; presence gating is not hiding the signal (the pre‑gating score scores worse); and novelty detection fails for a reason consistent with its own premise — a residual class has no compact region of feature space to be far from.

A negative control caught a confound we would have published

Run on random‑colour images, where no signal can possibly exist, the novelty detector scored 0.966. Cause: a region's mean colour has noise that shrinks as the region grows, so a "distance" feature partly measures region size, which correlates with the answer. Fixed with size‑stratified scoring and unit‑tested both ways (pure size‑leak 0.503; genuine signal 0.926). Without the control, an AUC near 1.0 would have gone into the paper as a breakthrough.

And a ground‑truth leak in our own code

The first version of the recovery evaluation scoped its regions to pixels the ground truth says are real classes. That hands the method advance knowledge of where to look, and immunity from damaging true background — exactly the protection that threshold relaxation does not get. It produced a clean, quotable +3.47 that was not a result. It is now kept deliberately as an explicitly labelled upper bound.

The mechanism, in one sentence

A catch‑all class gives the model a plausible answer everywhere, so a strong runner‑up carries no information. Where the vocabulary genuinely covers the scene, a strong runner‑up means a real class was suppressed — and the residual becomes detectable.

Two consequences we had to accept immediately: our own headline 29.68% is a LoveDA number, not a general one, and the presence‑collapse correlation is a property of LoveDA's annotation scheme rather than of SAM 3. Both are labelled as such throughout.

But recovery still failed — and the way it "worked" was a trap

Act V — The pivot

28 August · commits ca64d15 → 4e3a6c8

An earlier note in the project said "threshold tuning is closed". Re‑reading it, the evidence behind that sentence was a sweep of one global threshold, which found nothing (+0.04). Per‑class thresholds had never been tested. That oversight turned out to be the whole paper.

Step 1 — is there anything there? (the oracle bound)

First we cheated on purpose, to find the ceiling: choose the best possible threshold for each class using the answer key.

RungFree parametersLoveDA mIoU
Published threshold (0.5 for everything)047.37
Best single global threshold147.41 (+0.04)
Best per‑class thresholds648.83 (+1.46)

And crucially, the chosen thresholds spanned 0.170 to 0.595.

The insight the whole paper rests on

One global threshold is not merely suboptimal for different classes — it is wrong for them in opposite directions. That is why sweeping a single value finds nothing: raising it helps some classes exactly as much as it hurts others, and the average cancels.

water, which is right 89.5% of the time it fires but finds only half of what is there, wants a threshold of 0.170. It gains +6.70 IoU alone.

This was also the only gain in the project so far where land cover genuinely improves: the six real classes gained +8.63 in aggregate, against background's +1.59. Not the catch‑all artefact.

Step 2 — the deployable version

An oracle is not a method. The deployable version fits one threshold per class by coordinate ascent on a calibration subset, and evaluates on completely disjoint tiles.

Why this is not cheating

SegEarth‑OV3 already tunes its single threshold per dataset using labels — 0.5 for LoveDA, 0.1 for OpenEarthMap. ConInfer tunes theirs too (0.8 / 0.3 / 0.1). We use the same protocol with six parameters instead of one. No model weights are trained. It is parity, not a supervision leak — and the baseline is always recomputed on the very same held‑out tiles.

A bug the verification test caught

The bin‑edge calculation used truncation: 29/200 × 200 evaluates to 28.999999999999996, which truncates to 28. The function was scoring a threshold one bin below the one it reported. No recorded number changed — only 7 of 201 grid values were affected and none had ever been chosen — but a deployed threshold could have been wrong. That is precisely what this step exists to catch.

Step 4 — can we do it without labels at all?

The obvious next question. Eleven separate attempts, all failing, and the reason turned out to be more interesting than a twelfth attempt would have been.

Our initial explanation was: the oracle exploits per‑class precision, and precision requires labels by definition. We tested that instead of asserting it, using eight label‑free statistics.

Our stated reason was wrong, and the truth is stronger

Precision IS predictable without labels — four proxies rank the classes correctly, two of them at p ≤ 0.033. And it buys nothing. The best row in the table is below a random control's 95th percentile.

Because precision does not determine the threshold: the correlation between a class's precision and its ideal threshold is −0.429 with p = 0.419, and the relationship is not even monotone. water has the highest precision and the lowest threshold; road has middling precision and the highest threshold.

The real bound: the right thresholds solve a coupled multi‑class objective. Raising one class's threshold pushes pixels into background, which changes background's score, which changes every other class's optimum. No per‑class scalar can express that, measurable or not. This is exactly why a six‑parameter fit works and every one‑parameter rule fails.

That reframes the negative from "we could not measure the quantity" to "we can measure it, and it is the wrong quantity" — which explains eleven failures instead of merely recording them, and generalises to any training‑free pipeline that thresholds a per‑class score.

Worth noting: the cross‑head prediction was written down before the run — committed as "the one remaining candidate, and it will fail anyway, for the coupling reason". It turned out to be a good precision estimate and moved mIoU by +0.06. Exactly as predicted.

Act VI — From correlation to cause

We now had a method and a mechanism. Both had a soft joint, and a reviewer would find both. This act hardens them.

Soft joint 1: the +1.18 is a rural result

We also found the thresholds are genuinely domain‑specific — road wants 0.725 in rural and 0.225 in urban — and that applying the wrong domain's thresholds is worse than no calibration at all (both mismatched arms land below the published threshold). Pooling is not safe either: it fits one vector to two optima, which is the same failure as the global threshold, one level up. The method's own argument recurses on it. The practitioner rule: calibrate on the distribution you will evaluate on.

Soft joint 2: the mechanism was a correlation

Our strongest claim rested on comparing two datasets, and we had written in our own notes: "this is a stratification, not a randomised intervention". That sentence was the weakest joint in the strongest claim.

The fix came from noticing something about how the pipeline actually works.

The observation that made this free

In SegEarth‑OV3, every class is an independent forward pass with its own text prompt. There is no softmax, no normalisation, no interaction between classes anywhere — the only cross‑class operation in the whole pipeline is the final argmax.

Therefore dropping a class from the vocabulary is exactly equivalent to dropping its channel from a saved array. One cached run answers every vocabulary question. All thirteen experimental arms read identical model outputs and differ in nothing but the vocabulary — which is better than re‑running, because re‑running would have added sampling noise for nothing. Cost: zero GPU hours.

So we intervened on the causal variable directly: progressively merge real classes into the catch‑all, raising its share from 0.84% to 58.20%, and re‑measure detectability. Each dose has an arity‑matched control that merges the same number of classes into each other instead — so class count changes identically and only the catch‑all's share differs.

Twelve predictions were written into PREREGISTRATION.md and committed to git before each run, so the timestamps are independently verifiable.

SignalStart (0.84%)Largest dose (58.20%)ControlDose vs control
winning‑class confidence0.7940.5820.7102.5×

Both signals degrade causally with catch‑all share, and neither degradation is explained by class count. The intervention drove OpenEarthMap from 0.794 into the 0.58–0.62 band where LoveDA actually sits — it reproduced the observational regime rather than merely correlating with it.

Nine of the twelve predictions passed. We report the three failures. The dose–response is non‑monotone inside its range, one control moved slightly more than allowed, and the predicted rate missed at two of three doses. So: the direction transfers, the rate does not. All stated in the paper.

A near miss worth telling

The first verdict announced "CAUSAL" on the strength of an AUC dropping to 0.208. But AUC is symmetric: 0.208 is a detector of strength 0.792 with its sign flipped, not a destroyed signal. The score was rewritten as max(AUC, 1−AUC) everywhere — and on rescoring, that arm's signal was flat, not destroyed.

The diagnosis also revealed a design flaw: the original merge made the catch‑all a union of well‑detected prompts, which is unnaturally competent. We kept it (it was pre‑registered) and added a second family that drops the prompts and relabels the pixels — which is the real situation. That is the family the paper quotes.

The reverse experiment fails, and the failure is the finding

The mechanism predicted that removing the catch‑all from LoveDA's vocabulary should raise detectability. Prediction P8, committed in advance. It moved from 0.592 to 0.540 — the wrong way.

The dissociation

Every arm that changes the vocabulary without changing the label space moves ≤ 0.052. Every arm that raises the catch‑all's share of ground truth moves 0.213–0.323. The causal locus is the LABEL SPACE, not the prompt list.

Practical consequence: you cannot fix a catch‑all‑heavy benchmark by editing the prompt list. Removing the prompt does not remove the pixels that are genuinely none‑of‑the‑above; it only makes them unnameable.

We also learned the effect saturates by about 35% share, which honestly narrows the claim — LoveDA starts at 35.7%, already inside the saturated band, so its dose arm had no room to act. We say that rather than presenting a single clean monotone story.

The metric recommendation, and OpenEarthMap turning positive

We now had the catch‑all artefact measured in both directions on two datasets. That is enough to make a recommendation rather than a complaint.

The leverage, which is not obvious

mIoU is an unweighted mean over N classes, so the catch‑all owns exactly 1/N of the metric no matter how meaningful it is — 14.3% on LoveDA for a class the annotation guide defines as "everything else", and 11.1% on OpenEarthMap for a class covering 0.84% of the pixels. A 22.67‑point move in that one class is 3.24 mIoU before anything real has changed.

Recommendation: benchmarks with a catch‑all class should report mIoU over the real classes alongside the headline.

Recomputing every table both ways produced the best surprise of the project.

OpenEarthMap had been written up as flat and dismissed as "a benchmark artefact" for four days. Under the recommended metric it gains +1.75 — more than LoveDA does. Its headline decomposes exactly: +1.56 from the eight real classes, −1.26 from the catch‑all alone. The catch‑all was cancelling a real gain.

This closed the thinnest part of the method claim, and it cost hours of CPU time.

Two more negatives, both closed properly

Is there a label‑free rule for when calibration is worth paying for? This would have turned a scope limitation into a deployment criterion — "measure your discard rate; above X%, buy the labels". We stratified both datasets by a label‑free residual measure. LoveDA came out U‑shaped (the most reliable gain is in the lowest‑residual stratum), OpenEarthMap gave the opposite sign, and a random control moved nearly as far. No rule.

One bound explains both halves

You cannot pick the thresholds without labels (eleven attempts, with the coupling reason) and you cannot predict whether picking them will pay without labels either (two datasets, opposite signs). The ~200‑tile cost is irreducible: it buys the answer and the question at the same time. That is a cleaner closing statement than the deployment criterion we went looking for.

What is the rural/urban gap actually made of? Arithmetic, since mIoU is an unweighted mean: water (48%) and forest (37%) carry 85% of it. Across all twelve (domain, class) cells, the precision–recall gap ranks which classes move (ρ = +0.713, p = 0.013) — a measured confirmation of the method's stated mechanism — while precision alone explains nothing (+0.168, p = 0.60), the same lesson arriving independently.

But we report the failure first: water, the largest contributor, has effectively identical share (11.6% vs 11.8%) and precision–recall gap (+35.0 vs +34.4) in both domains, and an 8× different gain. So roughly half the gap still has no mechanism, and the paper says so before quoting the correlation.

Act VII — The competitor, and the best result in the project

1 September · commits d36ac53 → 2028f2a

ConInfer is the nearest published competitor. We cited it in the paper without a number, which is the first thing a reviewer asks about. One table row closes it.

The rule that governed the whole exercise

Nothing for ConInfer touches the working environment. Ever.

Their install instructions read conda create -n ConInfer followed by pip install -r requirements.txt — with no conda activate between them. Run as written with our environment active, that command installs into our environment and destroys the only working version combination in the project.

We wrote a setup script that refuses to run while the main environment is active, snapshots its package list, and can diff it afterwards. And a matching package list is necessary but not sufficient — the real gate is behavioural: the baseline must still produce 47.38.

What reproducing published work actually required

This is worth recording plainly, because it is a reproducibility observation rather than a complaint, and it is evidence the run is real:

  • A file their eval.py imports on line 4 is absent from their repository. Copied from the upstream project.
  • Three dependencies declared by neither their requirements nor the packages importing them.
  • Their segmentor hardcodes the first author's filesystem paths. We repointed them with a reversible patch that edits paths only.
  • Their pinned torch version cannot build on this machine, for exactly the reasons documented in week one. We used the stack that works and reported the deviation.
  • Their weights require a licence‑gated satellite‑pretrained backbone that returns HTTP 403.
A shortcut we deliberately refused

A converted copy of those weights exists in a different format. Loading it would have required hand‑writing a parameter remap — and a remap that is slightly wrong loads successfully with half the network still randomly initialised, producing a plausible number from a broken model with no way to detect it. We did not do it. That is precisely the failure the rule "reproduce their published figure first" exists to prevent.

The reproduction, reported honestly

The split that reproduces closely is the one we hold in full; the one that fails badly is the one we hold 77% of. That is not a coincidence, and it decided which comparison goes in the paper.

The framing this forces, and we insisted on it

Our numbers are 11.54 points above ConInfer's on LoveDA. Most of that margin is the backbone — SAM 3 at 1024² against CLIP ViT‑B/16 at 448² — not our contribution. Our contribution is the +1.16 over SegEarth‑OV3. Presenting the 11.54 as ours would be exactly the error we built a whole paper on catching. We should not make it after that.

Also reported alongside: they are ~10× faster at inference (2 minutes vs 24). That belongs in the comparison too.

And then the experiment that was worth more than the comparison row

The comparison answers "how do you compare to ConInfer?". But there was a better question the paper could not answer: is per‑class thresholding just a SAM 3 quirk?

ConInfer thresholds per‑class scores with a single global value — precisely the design our method replaces. So we cached their scores in our format (a read‑only wrapper, zero edits to their source), and pointed our existing fitting scripts at it, at their operating point of 0.8.

Both gates passed first: the instrumented run printed 36.99 exactly, and the scores landed inside the expected range.

2. ConInfer stops being a competitor. Our method composes with it: their pipeline goes 36.99 → 39.52 with our calibration on top. We do not beat the nearest published work — we improve it. That is a far better answer to "how is this different from ConInfer?" than any margin would have been.

The precise, falsifiable statement

The correction is not backbone‑specific — it is dataset‑specific, and the axis is the catch‑all's calibration. Where it is pathological (OpenEarthMap, ~17 IoU), the fit improves land cover on both and the headline on neither, because the catch‑all it must sacrifice owns a ninth of the metric.

So we rewrote it. The claim splits in two:

We did not go looking for a variable that fits three points. Any story invented now would be fitted to the data it must explain, and the entire value of the pre‑registration is that it was not. A falsified prediction, reported as falsified, is worth more to a reviewer than a repaired one.

The bug this dataset found

A silent failure that produced a plausible number

Two lines of the measurement code quietly encoded "the catch‑all is the lowest class value". True for LoveDA and OpenEarthMap. False for Potsdam, where clutter is the highest.

The result: every tile reported 0.00% discard while the aggregate printed a plausible 5.55% — computed over the wrong set of rows, with the catch‑all counted as a real class losing 34.68% of its pixels to itself. The headline even fell inside our pre‑registered bracket.

The tell was not the headline. It was "mean 0.00%, median 0.00%, max 0.00%" sitting next to a non‑zero total. Fixed, and verified to be a bit‑exact no‑op on the two datasets where the old code was right, so no earlier number changed.

This was the fourth dataset‑specific assumption to survive in code whose whole job is to read its class list from the data. A dataset whose catch‑all is not first was always going to find them.

What the state of the field means for us

Three readings, and they shaped every strategic decision after Act IV:

  1. The field has moved from "make CLIP dense" to "bolt a foundation segmenter on". That move happened in December 2025, three months before we started. Competing on architecture against groups with cluster‑scale compute, from one 70 W card, is the losing game.
  2. Inference‑time context modelling was claimed in March 2026. The original novelty of our problem statement had a competitor before we opened the file. Our differentiators — semantic rather than visual context, region rather than patch, corpus‑level rather than per‑scene — were real. Then we measured them and they were worth 0.3 points.
  3. What nobody is doing is the evaluation and calibration question. Everyone in this lineage tunes a global confidence threshold per dataset and mentions it in a config file. Everyone reports mIoU including a catch‑all class that owns 1/N of the metric. Nobody had asked whether that threshold is the right shape, or what the catch‑all is doing to the numbers. That empty ground is where we ended up — not by design, but by measuring carefully enough that the gap became visible.
Our comparative advantage, stated plainly

We cannot out‑compute the competition. What we can do is out‑measure them: reproduced baselines on three datasets, validation gates that must pass before any number is believed, negative controls, oracle bounds on every negative result, pre‑registered predictions, and a mechanism that explains results rather than reporting them. Every decision from Act V onward played to that.

How we worked — the rules that produced this

Two dozen individual measurements are less useful to explain than the handful of habits that generated them, especially since most of the habits were adopted after something went wrong.

1. Measure before deciding

Every design choice that survived was settled by a cheap experiment rather than an argument: boundary‑length adjacency, signed PMI, the hierarchical matrix, SLIC over connected components. The co‑occurrence premise was tested with a 40‑line script before any method was built on it — and the ceiling test that killed the method took an afternoon. Finding that out in week three instead of week eleven is the difference between a pivot and a disaster.

2. Validation gates are hard stops

Any instrumented run must still reproduce 47.37 mIoU and 29.68% discard exactly. If either moves, the patch changed behaviour and the measurement is void. This caught nothing dramatic — which is the point. It is why every downstream number can be trusted, and it is what let us treat a cached histogram as a measurement instrument after proving it matched eval.py to 0.04 per class.

3. Write down what the result will mean, before you have it

Used three times. The presence‑gating counterfactual had both readings written in advance — and the outcome matched neither cleanly, which made the gap between "the number I expected to be decisive" and "the number that actually decided it" impossible to paper over. The vocabulary intervention had twelve predictions committed to git. The Potsdam prediction was computed from labels alone and committed before any inference.

Pre‑registration is nearly free and it is the single strongest signal of good faith a reviewer can be given. Almost nobody in computer vision does it.

4. Always run the control that shows what "nothing" looks like

A random‑proxy control (95th percentile +0.58) is why we know the best label‑free rule at +0.42 is noise. Random‑colour images are why we caught a detector scoring 0.966 where no signal can exist. An arity‑matched control is what made the vocabulary experiment causal rather than suggestive. On OpenEarthMap the random strata moved nearly as far as the real ones — without the control, that would have been written up as a pattern.

5. Cache everything the GPU produces

One 24‑minute run wrote a per‑tile cache. After that, every threshold sweep, oracle bound, cross‑validation and vocabulary arm was a CPU pass measured in seconds. The thirteen‑arm causal experiment cost zero GPU hours because of this. On a 70 W card it is the difference between running the experiments and not.

6. Report both metrics, always

Adopted after the catch‑all artefact was measured in both directions. It converted a complaint into a recommendation other people can adopt, and it recovered a dataset we had filed as flat for four days.

7. Read the generated prose against its own table

The recurring failure mode of this codebase, stated for the record

Four separate incidents. A verdict that reasoned in prose about "higher" and "lower" and swapped both branches. A test that printed "414% of the change" because two contributions had opposite signs. A pass/fail gate that asked only "is the mean positive" and passed a result with three of five folds negative. A summary that quoted a LoveDA number on three other datasets' outputs.

In all four, the tables were correct. Only the generated text was wrong. The numbers were never at risk; the conclusions printed beside them were. Every verdict now gets read against its own table, and gates that can be phrased backwards get rewritten in a form that cannot be.

8. Push back on your own results hardest

Five findings were refuted by us before anyone else could:

Closing — what the project actually is

It started as a plan to rescue thrown‑away pixels using the spatial grammar of land cover. That plan is dead, and it was killed by our own measurement rather than by a reviewer: a perfect co‑occurrence matrix is worth 0.3 points over copying the biggest neighbour.

What survived is smaller, sharper and better supported. A single confidence threshold is the wrong shape for a multi‑class open‑vocabulary pipeline. It is wrong for different classes in opposite directions, which is why nobody found it by sweeping one value. Fitting one threshold per class, on the same labelling budget the baseline already spends, and training no weights at all, recovers a real gain — on two architectures, two methods, and three datasets, and it composes with the nearest published competitor instead of beating it.

Around it sits a measurement study with a causal experiment, a pre‑registered prediction that held, a pre‑registered prediction that failed and is reported as failed, and a metric recommendation that other people can adopt.

The one‑line version

We set out to fix what the model discards, and ended up fixing the rule that discards it.

The most valuable thing in the repository is not any single number. It is that every negative result carries an oracle bound saying how much was actually available, so a reader learns where the headroom is rather than only that we failed to reach it — and that five of our own findings were refuted by us, in writing, with the numbers that killed them.
