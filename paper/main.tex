\title{It's the Labels, Not the Model:\\
       Why Presence-Gated Segmentation Discards, and Why Recovery Doesn't Help}
\author{\todo{authors, affiliation}}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Training-free open-vocabulary segmentation pipelines built on SAM~3 gate their
predictions by a confidence threshold and assign everything below it to a
\bg{} class. We show this discards a great deal of real land cover ---
\discardPct{} of all real-class pixels on LoveDA, \discardPx{} in total, with
silence outnumbering misclassification \discardRatio{} --- and that the size of
this residual, together with whether it can be detected at all, is governed by
the dataset's \emph{label design} rather than by the model. Where \bg{} is a
catch-all covering \bgShareLove{} of the scene, the residual is large and no
signal separates it from genuine background (nine tested; best AUC \aucLove{}
against a \aucFloor{} floor); where the vocabulary covers the scene
(\bgShareOEM{}), only \discardOEM{} is discarded and the runner-up class score
separates it at AUC \aucOEM{}. Recovery is nonetheless not worthwhile in either
regime: it yields \recoverLove{}~mIoU on LoveDA, and on OpenEarthMap its
apparent \recoverOEM{} is entirely attributable to \bg{} ceasing to be
over-predicted (\oemBgGain{}) while the real classes lose \oemRealLoss{}~IoU.
A different correction does work. Fitting one threshold \emph{per class} rather
than one globally --- on \calibTiles{} labelled tiles, with no model weights
trained, the same protocol the baseline already uses to tune its single
threshold --- gives \methodGain{}~$\pm$~\methodSD{}~mIoU on LoveDA with
land-cover classes gaining \methodLand{}~IoU and the catch-all untouched at
\methodBg{}, capturing \oracleShare{} of an oracle bound of \oracleBound{}.
Finally, we show this bound is not reachable without labels, and identify why:
\nAttempts{} label-free rules fail, four of which provably estimate per-class
precision, because the optimal threshold solves a \emph{coupled} multi-class
objective that no per-class statistic can express.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Open-vocabulary semantic segmentation promises to label an image against a list
of class names in plain English, with no training on the target dataset. For
remote sensing this is unusually valuable: annotation is expensive, and it must
be redone for every new region and sensor. Recent work replaces the multi-model
CLIP stacks that dominated this area with SAM~3, whose presence, semantic and
instance heads can be combined into a single pipeline that reports strong
results across twenty datasets~\cite{segearthov3}.

That pipeline ends in a threshold. Class scores are gated by a presence score,
the highest-scoring class is taken per pixel, and any pixel whose winning score
falls below a threshold $\tau$ is assigned to \bg{}. The threshold is tuned per
dataset using labels, and everything below it is given up on.

We measure what that costs. On LoveDA, \best{\discardPct{} of all real
land-cover pixels --- \discardPx{} of them --- are assigned to \bg{}}, and
across every class discard outnumbers misclassification by roughly
\discardRatio{}. The dominant failure of this pipeline is not that it is wrong;
it is that it is \emph{silent}.

The natural response is to recover those pixels, and the natural objection is
that a lower threshold would do the same thing more cheaply. Neither works
(Table~\ref{tab:interventions}).
Lowering $\tau$ from $0.5$ to $0.1$ recovers two-thirds of the residual and
costs \tauCost{}~mIoU, at \tauRatio{} wrong pixels for every one right; removing
the presence gate costs \presCost{}. A region-level recovery method built on a
corpus-level semantic co-occurrence prior --- our original proposal --- adds
\priorGain{}~points over simply copying the largest neighbouring region, and a
\emph{perfect} co-occurrence matrix adds \priorOracle{}.

Running the same pipeline on a second dataset explains why, and the explanation
is the paper's main contribution. On OpenEarthMap, whose \bg{} class is a rare
genuine label rather than a catch-all, only \discardOEM{} is discarded, there
are no catastrophic tiles, and the residual is easy to detect --- the runner-up
class score reaches AUC \aucOEM{} where on LoveDA nine separate signals sit at
chance (Fig.~\ref{fig:auc}). \best{Detectability is a property of the annotation scheme, not of the
model.} A catch-all class gives the model a plausible answer everywhere, so a
strong second-place score carries no information about whether a real class was
suppressed.

That analysis points somewhere more productive than recovery. A single global
threshold is being asked to serve classes with very different precision--recall
profiles: on LoveDA, \texttt{water} is right 89.5\% of the time it
fires but finds only 54.7\% of what is present, while
\texttt{agricultural} is balanced. Fitting one threshold per class on
\calibTiles{} labelled tiles --- no weights trained, the same protocol the
baseline already uses for its single $\tau$ --- gives
\methodGain{}~$\pm$~\methodSD{}~mIoU, with land cover gaining \methodLand{} and
the catch-all class untouched.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{A mechanism.} The catch-all class's share of ground truth
        (\bgShareLove{} vs \bgShareOEM{}) tracks the residual's size, its
        detectability, the presence--discard correlation and the catastrophic-tile
        count across two datasets (Table~\ref{tab:mechanism}).
  \item \textbf{A method.} Calibrated per-class thresholds:
        \methodGain{}~$\pm$~\methodSD{}~mIoU, land cover \methodLand{}, catch-all
        \methodBg{}, \oracleShare{} of an oracle bound of \oracleBound{}, verified
        end-to-end in the pipeline.
  \item \textbf{A bound, with its reason.} \nAttempts{} label-free rules fail to
        reach that bound. Four provably estimate per-class precision, which we had
        supposed was the missing quantity; it is not.
  \item \textbf{A null-model correction} for co-occurrence analysis on segmentation
        masks, which refuted one of our own earlier findings and generalises beyond
        this setting.
\end{enumerate}

Every negative result is reported with an oracle bound --- what a perfect version
of the missing component would have bought --- so a reader learns where the
headroom is rather than only that we failed to reach it.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Training-free open-vocabulary segmentation for remote sensing.}
SegEarth-OV~\cite{segearthov} established the setting, introducing a feature
upsampler trained on unlabelled imagery to recover the spatial detail that
CLIP's $1/16$ downsampling destroys. Its successor,
SegEarth-OV3~\cite{segearthov3}, replaces the CLIP stack with SAM~3~\cite{sam3}
and contributes two components we inherit: \emph{dual-head mask fusion}, which
takes the elementwise maximum of the dense semantic head and the aggregated
instance head, and \emph{presence-guided filtering}, which multiplies each class
map by a scalar presence score before thresholding. It is our baseline
throughout, and we reproduce it to within rounding on both datasets.

\paragraph{Context at inference.} ConInfer~\cite{coninfer} is the nearest
competitor: it fits a Gaussian mixture over DINOv3 patch features and fuses the
resulting posterior with the vision--language prior, reporting $+2.80$~mIoU. Its
context is \emph{visual}, at \emph{patch} level, fitted \emph{per scene}. Our
original proposal was semantic context at region level mined corpus-wide, which
ConInfer's own limitations section names as future work. We report that proposal
as an ablation rather than a contribution, because we measured it and it does not
earn its place (Section~\ref{sec:built}). We note explicitly that ConInfer's gain
should \emph{not} be read as evidence that appearance features solve the
detection problem we identify: it performs context modelling, not
residual-class detection, and the two are different tasks.

\paragraph{Spatial context as post-processing.} Dense CRFs~\cite{densecrf} are
the classical answer to ``use spatial context to fix segmentation'', and remain
the baseline a reviewer will raise against any neighbour-propagation scheme. Our
findings are relevant to that family: we show that on a catch-all dataset the
limiting factor is not the propagation rule but the inability to identify which
pixels should be propagated to at all.

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Datasets.} LoveDA~\cite{loveda} validation, \loveTiles{} tiles at
$1024^2$ and 0.3\,m GSD, seven classes including \bg{}, evaluated at
the baseline's published $\tau=0.5$. OpenEarthMap~\cite{openearthmap} validation,
\oemTiles{} of the official 500 tiles, nine classes, at its published $\tau=0.1$.
We reproduce the baseline at \loveBase{}~mIoU on LoveDA against a published
\lovePub{}, and \oemBase{} on OpenEarthMap against a published \oemPub{}. At
77\% of the OpenEarthMap split the latter is a sanity anchor rather
than a reproduction gate --- the tile mix differs --- but a broken class-name to
label-value mapping would have landed far off in one direction, so it establishes
that the preparation is sound.

\paragraph{Instrumentation and its validation gate.} All measurements come from
an instrumented copy of the baseline segmentor that records, per pixel, the
winning class and score, the runner-up, the pre-gating fused score, and each
head's own top-1, plus the per-class presence score per sliding-window view.
\best{Every instrumented run must reproduce \loveInstr{}~mIoU and \discardPct{}
discard exactly}; a run that does not has changed behaviour and is rejected. This
gate is not a formality --- it is what establishes that the additions are
observation-only, and every number below inherits it.

\paragraph{A cached sufficient statistic.} Because the argmax does not depend on
$\tau$, the histogram $H[\text{gt},\text{pred},\text{conf bin}]$ is a sufficient
statistic for the confusion matrix at \emph{any} per-class threshold vector.
Caching it once turns every subsequent threshold sweep, oracle bound and
cross-validation into a CPU operation on a saved array rather than a GPU run. We
verify it against the deployed pipeline in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\section{Anatomy of the residual}
\label{sec:anatomy}

\paragraph{Two mechanisms, not one.} A real-class pixel can be labelled \bg{} in
two ways: its winning score falls below $\tau$, or \bg{} wins the argmax outright
at $\mathrm{conf}\ge\tau$. On LoveDA, \mechA{} of the residual is the first and
\mechB{} the second --- \mechBpx{} pixels in \mechBtiles{} tiles, \emph{all of
them water}, in large homogeneous scenes where the presence head fires for \bg{}.
The distinction matters because \best{the second group is unreachable by any
threshold}: lowering $\tau$ cannot change an argmax. Part of the residual lies
outside what threshold tuning can address at all.

\paragraph{Threshold relaxation is non-linear, and expensive where it matters.}
Lowering $\tau$ from $0.5$ to $0.3$ recovers 99M pixels for
$0.73$~mIoU; from $0.3$ to $0.1$ recovers a nearly identical count for $4.81$.
The residual is not a uniform mass: a shallow band sits just below the operating
point and a deep tail does not. Tracing the confusion matrix, the full sweep
recovers 85.3M correct agricultural pixels while creating
147.9M false ones --- \tauRatio{} wrong per right. A scalar
threshold carries no information about \emph{which} class a region plausibly is,
so it cannot recover selectively.

\paragraph{Presence gating is a correlate, not a cause.} Tiles that fail
catastrophically ($\ge$99\% discarded, $n=\catTilesLove{}$) have a
median maximum presence score of $0.273$ against $0.918$ on healthy tiles, and
the correlation over all \loveTiles{} tiles is \presCorrLove{}. On one such tile
the semantic head emits a $+10.13$ logit for \texttt{road} --- effective
certainty --- while the presence gate crushes the tile's best achievable score to
$0.076$. We proposed that gating was destroying recoverable evidence, and then
refuted it: with gating removed, recovery on those tiles is \emph{uncorrelated}
with how suppressed they were ($r=+0.018$), the same intervention pushes healthy,
barely-gated tiles from 0.46\% to 54.11\% discard, and
mIoU falls by \presCost{}. The correlation is partly mechanical --- since
$P_{\text{final}} = P_{\text{fused}}\cdot S_{\text{pres}}$, a low presence score
\emph{forces} discard whether or not gating is at fault --- and we report it as an
observation only.

That counterfactual produced an unexpected finding. \best{SAM~3 essentially never
detects \bg{}}: its median presence score is $0.022$, against $0.45$--$0.91$ for
every real class, because \bg{} is not a visual concept but the dataset's
catch-all. The gate's largest measured effect is therefore \emph{suppressing
\bg{}} so that $\tau$, rather than the argmax, governs background assignment ---
which inverts the motivation the baseline paper gives for it.

Table~\ref{tab:mechanism} and Fig.~\ref{fig:mechanism} are the paper's central
observation. Six quantities
measured independently, by the same code on the same pipeline, separate the two
datasets in the same direction, and they are ordered by a single property of the
\emph{annotation scheme}: whether the vocabulary covers the scene.

The explanation is direct. LoveDA's \bg{} absorbs a third of every image, so the
model always has a plausible answer available, and a strong runner-up score means
nothing --- it could indicate a suppressed real class, or a background region that
merely resembles one. Its runner-up AUC is $0.541$. OpenEarthMap's \bg{} is a rare
marker for genuinely unlabelable pixels, so a strong runner-up does indicate a
suppressed real class, and the same signal reaches \aucOEM{}.

\paragraph{Two of our own claims do not generalise, and we label them.} The
presence-collapse correlation of Section~\ref{sec:anatomy} (\presCorrLove{} on
LoveDA, \catTilesLove{} catastrophic tiles) becomes \presCorrOEM{} with
\catTilesOEM{} catastrophic tiles on OpenEarthMap. It is a property of LoveDA's
annotation scheme, not of SAM~3. Likewise \best{\discardPct{} is not a general
figure} --- the identical pipeline discards \discardOEM{} on OpenEarthMap. Both
are stated here rather than left for a reader to discover.

\subsection{Catch-all share, intervened on}
\label{sec:intervention}

The evidence so far is observational: across datasets and across LoveDA's urban
and rural strata, detectability tracks the catch-all's share. Stratification
cannot rule out everything those strata differ in. But catch-all share is not a
property one has to go looking for --- \best{it is set by the vocabulary}. So we
manipulate it.

Every class in the baseline is an independent forward pass with its own text
prompt, and the only cross-class operation in the pipeline is the \texttt{argmax}.
Dropping a class from the vocabulary is therefore \emph{exactly} equivalent to
dropping its channel, and one cached score stack answers every vocabulary
question. All \ivArms{} arms read identical model outputs, so they differ in the
vocabulary and in nothing else --- there is no run-to-run variation to absorb.

We raise the catch-all's share by removing classes from the vocabulary and
labelling their pixels catch-all, which leaves the catch-all its own single weak
prompt. This is the situation a dataset like LoveDA presents. \best{Each dose arm
is paired with an arity-matched control} that removes the same prompts but labels
those pixels a real class, so the class count changes identically and only the
share differs. Without that control the experiment measures ``fewer classes is an
easier problem''.

\paragraph{Detectability must be scored direction-agnostically.} AUC is symmetric,
so an AUC of $0.208$ is a detector of strength $0.792$ with its sign reversed, not
an absent signal. We report $\mathrm{det} = \max(\mathrm{AUC}, 1-\mathrm{AUC})$
throughout. This is not a technicality: under a variant that merges class
\emph{channels} into the catch-all rather than removing them, the top score does
not degrade at all --- it \emph{inverts}, because a catch-all assembled from
strong prompts is confidently correct about the pixels it absorbed. Scoring raw
AUC would have reported that as a destroyed signal.

Both signals degrade with the share, and neither degradation is explained by the
class count (Table~\ref{tab:intervention}). The intervention moves OpenEarthMap
from its own \ivA{} into the $0.58$--$0.62$ band where LoveDA sits, reproducing
the observational regime rather than merely correlating with it. Against the
weakest of the three controls rather than the matched one, the runner-up effect
is still \ivRatioWorst{}.

\paragraph{The label space is the lever, not the prompt list.} A second
intervention separates the two things the dose arms change together. Removing the
catch-all from LoveDA's \emph{vocabulary} while leaving its \emph{label space}
untouched --- \ivSatShare{} of ground truth is still catch-all, those pixels
merely become unnameable --- moves detectability from \ivRevBase{} to \ivRev{},
i.e.\ not at all. Across both datasets, \best{every arm that changes the
vocabulary without changing the catch-all's share of ground truth moves at most
\ivVocabMax{}, while every arm that raises that share moves \ivB{}--\ivBtwo{}}.
The practical consequence is that a catch-all-heavy benchmark cannot be repaired
by editing the prompt list.

\paragraph{The effect saturates, which bounds the claim.} It is concentrated at
the low-share end: above roughly \ivSatShare{} of the scene, four points across
both datasets sit within \ivSatLo{}--\ivSatHi{}, and further increases do nothing.
LoveDA \emph{begins} inside that band, so its own dose arm is underpowered rather
than null --- its dose and its control move by the same magnitude in opposite
directions. Two consequences follow, and we state both. The mechanism is
established where a catch-all grows from rare to roughly a third of the scene, and
not beyond. And \best{share therefore cannot explain the urban--rural gap} in the
stratified evidence, since both strata already lie in the saturated regime; that
stratification eliminates confusability, but the gradient within LoveDA remains
unexplained.

Predictions were registered in the repository before each run and are scored
against the outcome, including four that failed. The effect is
\emph{not} monotone in the interior of the dose range --- the smallest dose is the
minimum --- so we quote the endpoints. Point predictions from a line fitted to the
observational data missed at two of three doses, so the direction transfers and
the rate does not.

\section{What we built, and which parts are justified}
\label{sec:built}

Our original proposal was to recover the residual with a corpus-level semantic
co-occurrence prior: cut the discarded region into atoms, and label each atom by
conditioning on the classes of its neighbours through a matrix mined from the
model's own confident predictions. We report it here because two of its
components are worth keeping and the third is not.

\paragraph{The prior is real, and accurate.} Measured on ground truth, class
adjacency carries \pmiSignal{}~bits of mean absolute pointwise mutual information
against a structure-preserving permutation floor of \pmiFloor{} --- a factor of
\pmiRatio{}. Mined from the model's confident predictions with no labels, the
matrix agrees with the ground-truth matrix at Spearman $\rho=\rhoMined{}$, and
its error does \emph{not} concentrate on the classes the pipeline discards
($\rho=\rhoCirc{}$), which retires the obvious circularity objection with a
number.

\paragraph{A null-model correction, which refuted one of our own findings.}
Pointwise mutual information over class adjacency compares an observed
distribution against an independence baseline. The observation is a
\emph{boundary}-frequency distribution; the natural baseline is built from
\emph{area} marginals. These are different measures, and the mismatch
systematically inflates classes with high perimeter per unit area. Under the
area baseline we found, and believed, that \texttt{road} was a hub attracting
every other class. Under the boundary baseline \texttt{road}'s entire row
collapses to near zero and five class pairs change sign: we had been measuring
road's perimeter. We report the correction because it generalises to any
co-occurrence analysis over segmentation masks.

\paragraph{Atomisation dominates the prior.} Connected components of the
discarded region reach an oracle-labeller ceiling of \ceilCC{}; SLIC superpixels
reach \ceilSLIC{} (\ceilSLICoem{} on OpenEarthMap), because connected components
straddle class boundaries --- the largest was an entire tile
(Fig.~\ref{fig:purity}). Twenty ceiling points from the choice of atom.

\paragraph{The co-occurrence term does not earn its place.} Against simply
copying the largest neighbouring region, the mined prior adds \priorGain{}
points, and a \emph{perfect} ground-truth matrix adds \priorOracle{}. It helps
where four or more classes border a region ($+2.03$ mined, $+5.44$ oracle), but
that stratum is 10\% of the residual. The reason is structural
rather than a defect in the mining: classes that are adjacent are also the
classes that get confused, so the prior reinforces the very errors it was
intended to fix. We keep it as an ablation row.

\subsection{The method: per-class thresholds}
\label{sec:method}

A single threshold is being asked to serve classes with very different operating
points. On LoveDA, \texttt{water} has precision 89.5\% against
recall 54.7\%: it is right nine times in ten when it fires and finds
half of what is there, so it can afford to fire far more readily.
\texttt{agricultural}, at 66.9\% against 62.0\%, cannot.

We therefore fit one threshold per class by coordinate ascent on a calibration
subset of labelled tiles, and evaluate on disjoint tiles (Fig.~\ref{fig:method}). \best{No model weights
are trained.} The baseline already tunes its single $\tau$ per dataset using
labels --- $0.5$ for LoveDA, $0.1$ for OpenEarthMap --- so this is the same
protocol with $N-1$ free parameters instead of one, and the comparison is parity
rather than a leak. The published-$\tau$ baseline is recomputed on the same
held-out tiles.

\paragraph{The objective excludes the catch-all.} We fit to maximise mIoU over
the real classes only. This matters: optimising \emph{full} mIoU allows the search
to buy the metric by repairing an over-predicted background at land cover's
expense, which on OpenEarthMap trades \texttt{road} and \texttt{building} away for
a large \bg{} gain. Full mIoU is still what we report.

\label{sec:results}

\paragraph{LoveDA.} Five-fold cross-validation (Table~\ref{tab:method}) gives
\methodGain{}~$\pm$~\methodSD{}~mIoU with every fold positive and a worst fold of
\methodWorst{}. The gain is \best{entirely land cover}: the six real classes gain
\methodLand{}~IoU in aggregate while the catch-all moves \methodBg{}. That makes
the result structurally immune to the artefact described below, and it captures
\oracleShare{} of an oracle bound of \oracleBound{} --- little is left on the
table. It is driven by \texttt{water}, which gains \methodWater{}~IoU alone at a
fitted threshold of \methodWaterTau{} against a global $0.5$.

\paragraph{OpenEarthMap is a benchmark artefact, not a method failure.} Land
cover improves by $+12.45$~IoU in aggregate --- \texttt{building} $+4.35$,
\texttt{road} $+2.95$, \texttt{pavement} $+2.71$ --- and \bg{} pays for all of it
at $-11.04$, leaving full mIoU flat (the same shape as Fig.~\ref{fig:oemperclass}).
OpenEarthMap's \bg{} sits at $17.13$~IoU
with 17.33\% precision, so its mIoU is dominated by one
pathologically calibrated class and one can improve land cover \emph{or} the
metric, not both.

\paragraph{End-to-end verification.} The results above are computed from the
cached histogram. To confirm that this is a measurement instrument rather than
merely arithmetic, we deployed a single fit --- \calibTiles{} calibration tiles ---
in the actual pipeline. The prediction was \eeBase{}~$\to$~\eeFit{}~mIoU on
\eeTiles{} held-out tiles; the pipeline produced \eeBase{}~$\to$~\eeFit{}, with
every per-class delta agreeing to within $0.04$. Two honest notes. In this single
fit \bg{} gains $+0.85$~IoU, 10\% of the total rather than the
110\% of the artefact below, and incidental --- the objective
excludes it. And \texttt{road} \emph{loses} $0.53$: a per-class rule can hurt a
per-class result, which is why we report the full table rather than the mean.

\paragraph{The gain is not uniform, and the thresholds do not transfer.}
LoveDA's urban and rural splits differ by a factor of two in discard rate and
flip the sign of ten of fifteen class-adjacency pairs, so fitting within and
across them is closer to independent replication than to a re-split
(Table~\ref{tab:domain}). Two things follow, and both narrow the claim.
First, \best{the gain is a rural result}: \DOMr{}~$\pm$~\DOMrsd{}~mIoU with every
fold positive, against \DOMu{}~$\pm$~\DOMusd{} on urban, which is not
distinguishable from zero. The pooled \methodGain{} is carried by the larger
stratum, and we report it as a result on the benchmark as published rather than
as a claim about urban imagery. Yet land cover improves in \emph{both} domains
--- \DOMrreal{} and \DOMureal{}~IoU in aggregate --- and urban's catch-all loses
\DOMubg{}, which accounts for the flat result exactly. \best{This is the
OpenEarthMap artefact of Table~\ref{tab:oemperclass} with its sign reversed}, and
we take it as independent confirmation, arriving from the opposite direction,
that full mIoU is a poor metric wherever a catch-all class is large.

Second, the two domains want genuinely different thresholds --- mean absolute
difference \DOMtaudiff{}, and \DOMtaumax{} on \texttt{road}
($0.725$ rural against $0.225$ urban) --- and calibrating on the wrong one is
\best{worse than not calibrating at all}: \DOMxr{} and \DOMxu{}, both below the
published $\tau$. Pooling is not a safe default either, retaining \DOMpr{} of
\DOMmr{} on rural. \best{A calibration set spanning two domains fits one
threshold vector to two optima, which is the same failure as the global $\tau$ it
replaces, one level up.} The practitioner-facing rule is therefore narrow and
concrete: calibrate on the distribution you will evaluate on, and do not pool
across domains that differ this much.

\subsection{The label-free bound, and why it holds}
\label{sec:bound}

Sweeping a single global $\tau$ finds \globalOracle{}~mIoU on LoveDA, which is why
threshold tuning is usually considered closed. Sweeping a threshold \emph{per
class} is worth \oracleBound{} --- more than thirty times as much --- with the
real classes gaining $+8.63$~IoU. The chosen thresholds span \eeSpread{}: one
global value is wrong for different classes in opposite directions.

We tested \nAttempts{} label-free rules against that bound: three threshold rules
(per-class Otsu, a confidence percentile, and presence-scaled thresholds) and
eight per-class statistics converted into thresholds by a single fitted knob ---
the same one-parameter budget the baseline spends on its own $\tau$. \best{None
reaches it.} The best scores \proxyBest{}~mIoU against a random-proxy control
whose 95th percentile is \proxyCtrl{}; on LoveDA every one of the three threshold
rules scores \emph{below} the published $\tau$. The control is not optional: one
fitted knob over six classes can extract a gain from a meaningless vector.

\paragraph{The reason is not the one we first proposed.} We had supposed the
oracle exploits per-class \emph{precision}, which is label-derived by definition.
That is false. Precision is predictable without labels: mean confidence ranks the
six classes by precision at $\rho=\proxyConfRho{}$ ($p=\proxyConfP{}$), and
agreement between SAM~3's two heads --- available only because we instrument them
separately, since the baseline fuses them with a maximum --- at
$\rho=\proxyHeadRho{}$ ($p=\proxyHeadP{}$). Both $p$-values are exact, by
enumerating all $720$ relabelings, because at six classes $|\rho|=0.6$ arises by
chance roughly a fifth of the time. Both move mIoU by less than $0.1$.

\paragraph{The restated bound.} Precision does not determine the threshold. The
relation is not even monotone: \texttt{water} at 88.5\% precision
takes $\tau=0.175$, \texttt{road} at 68.5\% takes $0.675$, and
\texttt{barren} at 55.6\% takes $0.375$
($\rho=\proxyTauRho{}$, $p=\proxyTauP{}$). \best{The optimal per-class threshold
is the solution to a coupled multi-class objective}: raising one class's threshold
moves its pixels into \bg{}, changing \bg{}'s IoU and therefore every other
class's optimum. No per-class scalar can express that, measurable or not. This
explains why an $N$-parameter fit works and every one-parameter rule fails, and
it generalises to any training-free pipeline that thresholds a per-class score.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Two datasets.} The mechanism is established by intervention
(Section~\ref{sec:intervention}) on one of them, and the stratified evidence
supporting it comes from the other. A third dataset whose catch-all is common but
visually distinct would strengthen both, and we did not have one: the obvious
candidate, iSAID, has a catch-all that is 97.11\% of ground truth \emph{and}
maximally confusable, so it would confirm the ordering and discriminate nothing.
The doses in Section~\ref{sec:intervention} are chosen greedily from the smallest
classes upward rather than at random, and the arity-matched control is not
perfectly inert --- dropping a prompt disturbs the scores wherever that class's
pixels lie --- so we report the dose-to-control \emph{ratio} rather than the
difference alone.

\paragraph{Calibration must match the evaluation distribution, and the gain is
not uniform.} Fitting on LoveDA's \emph{train} split and evaluating on val gives
\trainToVal{}~mIoU, because those splits differ sharply in discard rate at
identical background share. Table~\ref{tab:domain} bounds this at a controlled
calibration budget: thresholds fitted on one LoveDA domain and applied to the
other give \DOMxr{} and \DOMxu{}~mIoU, \emph{below} the published $\tau$, so the
wrong domain's calibration is an active harm rather than a diminished benefit.
The method requires calibration data from the deployment distribution, and
\calibTiles{} tiles of it; below fifty tiles it actively hurts. Within LoveDA the
gain is also concentrated: \DOMr{} on rural against \DOMu{} on urban, so our
headline \methodGain{} is a benchmark-level figure and not a claim about every
stratum of it. We regard this as the sharpest limitation of the work: the method
is a calibration correction, and calibration is exactly the thing that does not
transport.

\paragraph{Oracle bounds are bounds under our labeller.} Every ``oracle'' figure
we report gives one component perfect information while holding the rest of our
implementation fixed. They bound what our design could achieve, not what is
achievable in principle.

\paragraph{Scope.} OpenEarthMap is evaluated at \oemTiles{} of 500 tiles.
Recovery is evaluated at one threshold per dataset --- each dataset's own tuned
value --- though Section~\ref{sec:bound} bounds what any other threshold could
have given. Deep features remain untested for detection; we judged this
unnecessary because detection already succeeds on OpenEarthMap (AUC \aucOEM{})
and recovery still fails there, so better detection buys nothing.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The residual that presence-gated segmentation pipelines discard is a property of
the annotation scheme as much as of the model, and whether it can be detected
follows the same axis. Neither regime makes recovery worthwhile: where the
residual is hard to find it cannot be found, and where it is easy to find the
resulting labels are not accurate enough to beat leaving it alone. What does work
is cheaper and less interesting to describe --- fitting the threshold per class
rather than globally --- and it works because a single threshold is being asked to
serve classes with incompatible operating points. We give the bound on that idea,
show it cannot be reached without labels, and identify why: the quantity that sets
the right threshold is not a per-class property at all.

We would emphasise one methodological point. Three of the results in this paper
are refutations of our own earlier claims, each caught by a test written on
purpose --- a null model that inverted a published finding of ours, a supervision
leak that produced a plausible and quotable number, and a detector that scored
$0.966$ on random noise. Reporting a headline mIoU gain without decomposing it
per class is, on this evidence, the single easiest way to publish something that
is not true.
