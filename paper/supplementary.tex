\section{The co-occurrence prior, in full}
\label{sup:prior}

Our original proposal was to recover the residual with a corpus-level semantic
co-occurrence prior: cut the discarded region into atoms and label each by
conditioning on its neighbours through a matrix mined from the model's own
confident predictions. The prior is real: class adjacency carries \pmiSignal{}~bits of mean absolute
pointwise mutual information against a structure-preserving permutation floor of
\pmiFloor{}, a factor of \pmiRatio{}. Mining it without labels reproduces the
ground-truth matrix at Spearman $\rho=\rhoMined{}$, with error that does
\emph{not} concentrate on the classes the pipeline discards ($\rho=\rhoCirc{}$),
which retires the circularity objection with a number.
\best{It still does not earn its place.} Against copying the largest neighbouring
region it adds \priorGain{} points, and a \emph{perfect} ground-truth matrix adds
\priorOracle{}. The reason is structural rather than a mining defect: classes
that are adjacent are also the classes that get confused, so the prior reinforces
the errors it was meant to fix. We keep it as an ablation.

\paragraph{A null-model correction, which refuted one of our own findings.}
Pointwise mutual information over class adjacency compares an observed
distribution against an independence baseline. The observation is a
\emph{boundary}-frequency distribution; the natural baseline is built from
\emph{area} marginals. These are different measures, and the mismatch
systematically inflates classes with high perimeter per unit area. Under the area
baseline we found, and believed, that \texttt{road} was a hub attracting every
other class; under the boundary baseline its entire row collapses to near zero
and five class pairs change sign. We had been measuring road's perimeter. The
correction generalises to any co-occurrence analysis over segmentation masks.

\paragraph{Atomisation dominates the prior.} Connected components of the
discarded region reach an oracle-labeller ceiling of \ceilCC{}, because they
straddle class boundaries --- the largest was an entire tile. SLIC
superpixels~\cite{slic} reach \ceilSLIC{} (\ceilSLICoem{} on OpenEarthMap).
Twenty ceiling points from the choice of atom, against \priorOracle{} for a
perfect prior.

\paragraph{The gain is not uniform, and the thresholds do not transfer.}
LoveDA's urban and rural splits differ by a factor of two in discard rate and
flip the sign of ten of fifteen class-adjacency pairs, so fitting within and
across them is closer to independent replication than to a re-split
(Table~\ref{tab:domain}). Two things follow, and both narrow the claim.
First, \best{the gain is a rural result}: \DOMr{}~$\pm$~\DOMrsd{}~mIoU with every
fold positive, against \DOMu{}~$\pm$~\DOMusd{} on urban, which is not
distinguishable from zero. The pooled \methodGain{} is carried by the larger
stratum, and we report it as a result on the benchmark as published rather than
as a claim about urban imagery. Yet land cover improves in \emph{both} domains
--- \DOMrreal{} and \DOMureal{}~IoU in aggregate --- and urban's catch-all loses
\DOMubg{}, which accounts for the flat result exactly. \best{This is the
OpenEarthMap artefact of the OpenEarthMap per-class figure in the main paper with its sign reversed}, and
we take it as independent confirmation, arriving from the opposite direction,
that full mIoU is a poor metric wherever a catch-all class is large.

\paragraph{What the gap is made of.} Because mIoU is an unweighted mean over
classes, the difference between the domains decomposes exactly into per-class
contributions, and it is concentrated: \texttt{water} accounts for \cpWater{} of
it and \texttt{forest} for \cpForest{}, \cpTop{} between them. Across all twelve
(domain, class) cells, the statistic that ranks which classes move is the
\best{precision--recall asymmetry} ($\rho=\cpGapRho{}$, permutation
$p=\cpGapP{}$), not precision itself ($\rho=\cpPrecRho{}$, $p=\cpPrecP{}$) --- the
same lesson as the label-free bound in the main paper, arriving independently. This is the
mechanism the method assumes, measured rather than argued: a class that is right
far more often than it fires can afford a lower threshold. \texttt{forest} is the
clean case, with the two domains in opposite regimes (recall $9.9$ against
$68.9$, discard $67\%$ against $12\%$).

\best{It does not, however, explain \texttt{water}}, which is the largest single
contributor. Its share of each domain ($11.6\%$ against $11.8\%$) and its
precision--recall gap ($+35.0$ against $+34.4$) are effectively identical, yet its
IoU gain differs eightfold. So the asymmetry ranks the classes on average while
roughly half of the gap still lacks a mechanism. We suspect the coupling described in the main paper: in the urban domain, lowering
\texttt{water}'s threshold takes pixels from the dense \texttt{building} and
\texttt{road} classes, so the same move buys less. We have not measured this and
do not claim it.

Second, the two domains want genuinely different thresholds. The mean absolute
difference is \DOMtaudiff{}, rising to \DOMtaumax{} on \texttt{road}
($0.725$ rural against $0.225$ urban), and calibrating on the wrong one is
\best{worse than not calibrating at all}: \DOMxr{} and \DOMxu{}, both below the
published $\tau$. Pooling is not a safe default either, retaining \DOMpr{} of
\DOMmr{} on rural. \best{A calibration set spanning two domains fits one
threshold vector to two optima, which is the same failure as the global $\tau$ it
replaces, one level up.} The practitioner-facing rule is therefore narrow and
concrete: calibrate on the distribution you will evaluate on, and do not pool
across domains that differ this much.
